The first time I listened to *Discipline*, “Matte Kudasai” grabbed me by the ears.

I was busy with other things, the music merely playing in the background—a habit I’ve increasingly adopted for music appreciation in recent years, waiting for the right moment to give an album a dedicated listen. But when the record reached this track, I noticed it almost instantly. I added it to my playlist straight away, telling myself, *Revisit this one properly next time.*

There’s no other reason: its character is so distinct, it hardly even sounds like something King Crimson should have written.

Later I played it on repeat, trying to take in its introspective, ethereal, contemplative, and melancholic atmosphere. I tried to get used to Adrian Belew’s hardly surprising, Westernized Japanese pronunciation. Most importantly, I was trying to understand why King Crimson would craft such a song about waiting, one that sounds so pop, especially considering what *Discipline* is famous for.

Why does King Crimson speak Japanese?

For the longest time, I had no answer. Back then, my take was that there was no take—just like any rock band, a flash of inspiration, a spontaneous spark—they wrote it because they felt like it. Sometimes a song just ends up on an album by chance, without any particular meaning.

Until, eventually, waiting long enough produced a hypothesis.

I was first drawn in by the atmosphere, so that’s where I want to start. The song’s mood-setting works remarkably well. Without much effort, I can easily conjure a beautiful image: a sunset pouring in through the window and filling the hall, a woman leaning against the wall, head turned, gazing out the window. Time seems to suspend itself; the golden sunset remains forever, neither sinking nor rising.

The lyrics sustain this melancholy air, as if telling the story of a long wait—one that could almost be interpreted as a love song.

But why, of all languages, Japanese?

King Crimson is a British band; their native tongue is English. The location hinted at in the lyrics is America, which also speaks English. The theme is waiting. In every respect, there seems to be no reason Japanese had to be used. “Matte Kudasai” itself comes from the Japanese “待ってください”—a very simple, very everyday expression with no particular meaning. Yet, this Japanese phrase floats alone in a song full of English, offering no concrete Japanese flavor, but rather serving only as a jarring ornament.

“Please wait.”

So I tried looking for clues outside the song. My gaze settled on the album’s release year: 1981. I closed my eyes, recalling everything I’d read about the West and the East in the 80s. Ideas began to take shape.

By 1981, the Western world held complicated feelings about Japan—and indeed, of East Asia as a whole. Japan in the 70s and 80s was no longer the distant, mysterious Orient, nor the submissive image of a defeated WWII nation. It had become a highly industrialized, modern economic powerhouse. The scale of its manufacturing and consumption, represented by automobiles and electronics, made Japan a singular entity: at once unfamiliar and hyper-modern; bearing the heterogeneity of Eastern culture while pushing Western-originated modern industrial civilization to a level that was awe-inspiring—even vaguely unsettling. The Western attitude toward Japan was thus inherently contradictory: anticipation and caution, curiosity and unfamiliarity coexisted. They were forced to look for their own familiar modernity in this “Orient”. Japan was both the Other and a mirror of the modern world.

With this in mind, the lyric “her sad America” suddenly jars. If this were a standard song of pining, why must “America” appear? Why personify America as “her sad America”?

To me, this “sad America” offers a new perspective for reading the song—a mourning for American and Western modernity. Crucially, this is not anti-Americanism. It isn’t saying, “Damn America.” Instead, it uses America to represent a world that is aging, a world once so important, now, though, drifting into sadness.

“Waiting” thus gains a new meaning. In English, the word “wait” appears in one line: “She waits in the air.” This can be interpreted in various ways. Taken literally, she is waiting in mid-air. But as a metaphor, it could mean “she waits in suspense” or “she waits in an atmosphere that suffuses everything”. The latter reading is well worth dwelling on. It may not just be one person waiting for another; it could be one culture waiting for another, or perhaps a dying world helplessly waiting for the return of a past it can no longer reclaim. Who is saying “待ってください” to whom? Is it the West waiting for a world charging ahead, or the new East waiting for the aging West? The song provides no answer.

Some might argue, “You’re overthinking it.” That this was simply because, in the 80s, everything Japanese was trendy—a passing, unplanned move by the band.

But as an Easterner, I cannot resist the temptation of this interpretation. Traditionally, the West has often placed the East in the position of being watched: mysterious, ancient, strange, dangerous, yet alluring. But here, the gaze is inverted. A Western rock song bearing an Eastern name begins to watch America in return. The Western world, led by America, becomes the object being watched, felt, and mourned. Thus, Japanese here may not just represent Japan, but rather a heterogeneous modernity entering the Western field of vision.

The album’s sequence also supports this hypothesis. Listening to “Matte Kudasai” in isolation, it truly seems like a pop song that doesn’t belong in a King Crimson record. It is soft, dreamlike, melodically beautiful, and clashes with the rest of the album. I tried deleting it from the playlist, letting “Frame by Frame” bleed directly into “Indiscipline”—it didn’t feel “wrong”. But if we keep the original tracklist and widen our lens to the connections between the other songs and this one, you’ll find: “Frame by Frame” is precise, repetitive, mechanical, two guitars spinning like high-speed flywheels. Then, “Matte Kudasai” brings it all to a sudden halt, the music turning soft, damp, and melancholy—like the whole world quieting down for a moment before the sun dips below the horizon. Next, “Indiscipline” exposes the psychological tension, paranoia, and loss of control of an individual. Finally, “Thela Hun Ginjeet” throws us entirely into city streets to face conflict, turning the once-refined modern world into a frenzied jungle (“Heat in the jungle”).

Thus, if I had to sum up “Matte Kudasai” in one word, it would be the “sunset” I mentioned earlier. A sunset is not doomsday. Doomsday means everything is over; a sunset means the world is still beautiful, but it suddenly reminds you how transient that beauty is. What moves me about this song is its restrained sadness. It creates a space where time stands still and tells me: nothing has happened, yet something is quietly about to leave.

The late 70s West went through the Vietnam War, oil crises, economic stagflation, the aftermath of counterculture movements, radical changes in the social structure, punk, the urban crisis, and Cold War anxiety. The idealism of the 60s—the belief that rebellion, liberation, and cultural revolution could create a new world—was already showing signs of fatigue. The hippie era’s utopian fantasy didn’t bring a brave new world; it left behind confusion, disappointment, and spiritual emptiness. As the Eagles sang in “Hotel California”, “We haven’t had that spirit here since 1969.” In stark contrast, another world was striding into modernity. The sun was setting in the West; the beast of the East had just pulled onto the highway.

These images form a fascinating correspondence with the musical aesthetic of *Discipline*. On this album, King Crimson explored unprecedented ways of performance and musical interaction—precision, repetition, geometry, mechanics; essentially, an acoustic translation of “discipline”. They successfully broke through the traditional dual-guitar format, where the two players no longer represented melody and harmony separately, but rather interlocked, each within the other, with neither precedence nor hierarchy, to construct extremely precise grooves, making it feel less like musicians playing music and more like music playing the human body.

Order, discipline, mechanics—these are the most distinctive features of industrialization and modernity, and the impression that beast gives. Was the West not once the same? Yet today, “discipline” seems to have become a good remedy for saving oneself from a degraded reality.

I want to mention one more thing I admire about this album. I’ve discussed many serious facts that weigh heavily, and the music seems to carry a particularly weighty significance. But what I appreciate most is that the creators didn’t offer any judgment on the scenes they described. They didn’t try to tell the listener which world was better, nor did they declare which would inevitably fail. They simply, faithfully rendered everything they saw, along with the internal tension and anxiety.

Perhaps this is the song’s most mature quality. If King Crimson had truly wanted to write a social allegory, they could easily have written a manifesto about the rise of the East and the fall of the West—they were capable of writing something better than that—but they didn’t. Even if the West is degenerating, the beast of the East is not a savior; it’s just another thing arriving. As for what it brings, the song takes no responsibility for answering. So, despite the many political interpretations I’ve made here, when I return to listening, I’m still reluctant to attach too much political meaning to it. In the end, “Matte Kudasai” feels more like an artist observing the world at a moment of dramatic transformation, seeing all the myriad scenes. He made no speech and passed no judgment—he just watched, and then he sighed.

That sigh is this song.

They are a group of people standing on the border, able to see both sides at once. An old world losing its light; an unfamiliar new world having crossed the sea. They watch it unfold unstoppably under the sunset, and finally, someone in the crowd shouts a vague request: *Please wait.*

Just as that pale request could not hold back the tide of the times, this song does not bring *Discipline* to a close. After a brief respite, “Indiscipline” begins. The most exciting part of the album is still waiting ahead. As for what the future holds, King Crimson doesn’t say. They just turn away, and keep playing.
