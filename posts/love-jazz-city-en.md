The story of *La La Land* and me began in high school.

It was the winter break of my first year of high school, and the small subtropical city in the southwest had been swallowed whole by a biting wind. Winter here stays above zero, but together with the damp climate and the absence of hot-water radiators, it still makes you curl up involuntarily. I dragged a long electric heater into my room and burrowed into the blankets as well, waiting for the room to warm up slowly.

What was I thinking about then? Probably savoring the various excitements of starting high school; I was getting ready to watch *Neon Genesis Evangelion* properly, a recommendation from a new friend. And most important of all, I was full of delight and anticipation about the photography club I had just joined.

Yes—the photography club is exactly the part of my memory I was looking for.

Through a New Year’s Day get-together, I got talking to a girl in the club who was in my year. The reason the two of us actually started chatting was as corny as something the worst novelist would invent: she had only come to confirm the activity arrangements with me, and I had only answered as usual—except that back then I had a habit of sprinkling emoji into my messages.

And she noticed. She asked me why I liked sending them. A little later, she said she had never realized these little faces were so cute. Saying so, she began sending them too.

And so we fell into talking. Our conversation soon turned to musical tastes. I must have seen some omen at the time, and so I sent her a pile of names of Western pop musicians. All of them were ones I liked.

Here is the most magical part. Even as I sent them, before she had replied, I could already picture her delighted expression at the other end. Her mouth opening slightly, an involuntary “Wow” escaping.

And just like that, we became close friends. That winter break seemed so long, and she and I had plenty of time to waste on discussing art and literature.

So she pulled me along to watch films together across the distance, using an app’s “screening room” feature and staying on the phone the whole time.

The first film we watched together was one she recommended. It was called *La La Land*.

I cannot remember whether we actually finished that film together. But what I still remember is that within the first half hour, I knew I liked it.

And she will never know that one offhand recommendation of hers changed another person’s life completely.

# Love

When *La La Land* was first released in mainland China, I was still in elementary school.

At the time I was in an interest class the school ran—photojournalism (a coincidence, always photography). The teacher was an interesting person with a rich life behind her, and, I suppose, something of an artsy young woman. In class she talked with us about film with great relish, as if a room of fourth and sixth graders could understand her. I do not blame her, though. I have always thought that not losing your enthusiasm for talking freely just because of your audience’s age is an excellent quality.

Back to the film. The distributor that year chose to release it on 14 February, which really was a cruel joke. Learning about this arrangement years later, I can imagine couples all over the streets buying into the film’s marketing, walking into the cinema full of anticipation, and ending up with a bad ending. But the studio’s reasoning was perfectly sound. It really is a film about love, no mistake about it.

Except that Mia and Seb’s love is out of the ordinary. Their first meeting began with road rage on the highway and ended at the moment Mia, drawn by the piano, walked into the restaurant. Later I learned that this is the classic Hollywood plot pattern that arose in the 1930s—the bickering couple, also called screwball comedy. Their time together is spent, from the very beginning, in the tension of opposition and conflict. Mia is a “would-be actress” working in a café, her dreams stymied again and again, sensitive and stubborn; Seb cannot even hold down a job, stubborn and aloof, and his understanding and acceptance of reality may be even less than Mia’s.

At first glance their fields do not intersect at all. Mia’s knowledge of jazz stops at the superficial view of the general public, and she even says she hates it. When Seb drags her to the Lighthouse Café and pours out all his jazz knowledge and his pessimistic take on the music onto Mia in one go, he looks exactly like a nerd who has not found anyone to talk to in a long while.

But they both happen to have dreams.

It took them no effort at all to discover each other’s defining trait—enthusiasm for dreams. Similar people always find each other quickly. So for quite a while, Mia and Seb’s love was nearly ideal: mutually supportive, aesthetically in tune, genuinely glad for each other’s dreams. Their first date came about precisely because of the phone call telling Mia about an audition.

And so, when the two of them sit at the piano with the hazy green light coming through the curtains behind them, the beauty of love reaches its peak.

It is also from here that the film “betrays” the screwball comedy pattern.

That green light, slightly exaggerated in color, comes from Hitchcock’s *Vertigo*. The director also deliberately dressed Mia in purple to match Madeleine, the female lead of *Vertigo*. To my mind such an arrangement is more than a homage; it also creates a dreamlike atmosphere with melancholy hidden inside it. Even though the two are deep in love at this point, the montage surrounding this scene already hints at conflict brewing: intercut with the scene are Mia and Seb each busy with their careers, their schedules no longer in sync, their communication gradually thinning. And after *City of Stars* ends, the very next scene is *Start a Fire*, which plunges the female lead completely into doubt. After that, every conflict that wounds their relationship arrives at once.

And I have to admit, the film’s gradual descent into sadness is exactly what I like about it.

I have no particular preference for tragedy over comedy, but I have always admired realistic, mature works. I have seen many comments lamenting Mia and Seb’s ending enormously, some even convincing themselves that the fantasy montage at the close is the real ending. I do not think so. Of course I feel sad that the two leads do not end up together, and sometimes I even cry over it, but at the same time I firmly endorse the ending that actually happens. I think it is a sign of mature storytelling. I can understand why people are dissatisfied—Mia and Seb have only just confirmed to each other on a bench at Griffith Observatory that “I’m always gonna love you (too)”, and the next shot shows the female lead with a family of her own.

Is this a betrayal of their vow? No. Is it a betrayal of the classic Hollywood plot trajectory? Yes. And I welcome that betrayal. What the two of them, eyes on each other, say on that bench is of course sincere, but I would ask everyone to remember that the love between the two leads has something transcendent about it. They are both people who love their dreams and strive to chase them; they cannot possibly let their romantic life interfere with each other’s wholehearted commitment when a major opportunity appears, nor can they stop helping each other—or start cursing each other—the moment they fall out. Mia and Seb love each other, but they also love the figure the other cuts, never once looking back, while chasing a dream, and they love the passionate version of themselves that exists alongside a lover who holds grand dreams.

Seb loves Mia, so he loves her dream too, so he has to let her go.

The film simply inserts a five-year gap, and that is not for lack of reasons why the two must part. Some analyses say the two leads had ways of staying together, that they did not have to separate, suggesting the director forced a bad ending. I think it shows **impermanence**. Other people’s analysis may be right; Mia and Seb did have a way to keep loving each other. But looking back at that day, at Griffith Observatory in winter, Seb resolved to let her go to Paris alone, and Mia resolved to part from Seb. In that instant, you really cannot persuade them to consider other possibilities. A difference of a single thought changed the trajectory of a life—this is the impermanence of fate.

These lessons I did not understand in fourth grade, and I still did not understand them in my first year of high school.

She pulled me into watching films over the phone, and somewhere in my heart I was probably secretly pleased. In fact, that winter break she and I often talked on the phone late into the night, until three or four in the morning. I cannot remember what we talked about; maybe it can only be summed up as talking about everything under the sun. But as time went by, I had to face a question I should have answered long before: do I like her?

Yes, I admitted to myself.

And her? I did not actually know. But we did talk about love. Once she told me quite bluntly that she could only accept someone whose first love she would be.

I froze. Because in an earlier conversation I had already told her that I had been in a relationship once before.

But I gave no particular sign of it. In the days that followed I simply pretended not to know this, and went on being good friends with her.

Until the winter break ended and the new term began, and then suddenly, from one day, she stopped replying to my messages, no matter what I sent her.

I was very sad.

I had never been through anything like it. I did not know what had happened, or what I had done wrong. The last message I sent her was an emoji with tears in its eyes.

There is no point in agonizing over whether she did it on purpose. I only told myself: this is impermanence.

# Jazz

Music is the other indispensable part of the film, and the part I love most deeply.

You cannot talk about *La La Land*, of course, without talking about jazz. It is what Seb has poured years of his life into, the most important driving force of this character. At the same time, as a musical, jazz also propels the entire film. From the very first shot, it sets the tone for the whole thing.

Many of the song-and-dance sequences use long takes, and the filmmakers’ camera choreography has reached the level of mastery. Music, in concert with the camera’s movement and the actors’ dancing, always carries the audience swiftly into flow. Add the vivid colors—arguably bizarre, yet never jarring—and arriving at each musical sequence is like opening a gift box packed with lovely presents from family and friends.

I cannot say which song-and-dance sequence is my favorite, because I do not want to give the answer “I like them all”. But if the question becomes “which one best represents the film”, then I would say *Another Day of Sun*.

> I think about that day
>
> I left him at a Greyhound station west of Santa Fe
>
> We were seventeen, but he was sweet and it was true
>
> Still, I knew what I had to do

From the music to the dancing it brims with cheer, and yet the lyrics cannot help revealing a faint melancholy. Cheering yourself on in the pursuit of a dream while quietly missing the person you had to leave behind. This song is, in effect, the whole film in miniature.

If Mia fell in love with jazz because of Seb, then so did I. Before this I did not know what jazz was, nor that I would like it. As Mia says, it is the music that plays on the radio and at parties, but you do not pay attention to it. Yet *La La Land* makes jazz the absolute protagonist for 128 minutes by way of a form—the film musical—that stands out as singular in the 21st century. You cannot ignore it, and if you stay, the odds are good that you will fall in love with it.

I have to admit that I was still quite naive about the arts back then. Where music was concerned, I had listened to Western pop for years, but pop was all it was. More importantly, I had no concept of a “genre” in my head at all. You could say jazz came crashing into my ears all at once.

And I savored every drop.

For two whole years after that winter break I was madly obsessed with *La La Land*. I downloaded the enormous Blu-ray disc image onto the computer in the club office and opened it up whenever I had the chance; I listened to the film’s soundtrack album over and over, until I could reconstruct on the strength of the music alone what was on screen at that moment. I brought it up with everyone around me again and again and ran shot-by-shot breakdowns for my club members, until everyone knew it was my favorite film.

I cannot quite explain why I loved *La La Land* so much in those days. I just felt that every part of it was beautiful, so beautiful that I was willing to repeat it over and over. If I had no time, I would skip straight to the song-and-dance sequences; if I had time, I would watch the whole thing minute by minute.

My appreciation of music advanced by great strides in high school as well. I soon plunged headlong into a musical world far broader than Western pop. For the first time I had a concept of genre; for the first time I felt a powerful urge to know what style the music flowing into my ears was, and what history it carried; I was delighted to discover that the kinds of music I could accept were so numerous after all, and that this abundance in turn enriched my inner world enormously. The time I spent with headphones on grew longer and longer, and my exploration inward went deeper and deeper.

And all of it began in that period when I was still in touch with her.

In one of our nighttime phone calls before she and I actually watched a film together, I mentioned that a new friend had recommended *Neon Genesis Evangelion* to me.

Then you should listen to Hikaru Utada’s *One Last Kiss*, she said.

I did. In less than a minute I was shaken from head to toe. I knew that for this song alone I would watch that anime to the end.

In the years since, I have received many music recommendations from this person or that, but whatever I hear when I open them, nothing has ever brought back the thrill that Hikaru Utada gave me back then.

Later still, I finally could not resist the temptation to make music with my own hands, and I took up an instrument too; like Seb, I began to harbor a small musical dream of my own.

I had assumed this was no more than adding another hobby, like photography before it. It was not until I had kept up self-study for two years that a chance event showed me, profoundly, how much weight that small dream carried.

That time my instrument had to be sent away for repairs, and I spent three whole weeks waiting for him to come back to me. By the second week alone, unable to play every day as usual, I had sunk into serious panic. On the surface this person was entirely normal; inside, besides the torment, there was doubt about life itself. In the days without an instrument, no amount of listening could make up for it.

Only then, slow as I was, did I realize that playing was my medicine.

By this point it is hard to say how much this still has to do with her. But I cannot help thinking that she did more than simply introduce me to a film.

And to this day I keep hoping that the thrill of that night will come again.

# City

*La La Land* shows Los Angeles in all its variety.

Not only the city under daylight sun, but the city under the stars at night, and, between the two, most important of all—the city in the blue hour.

The blue hour is the brief 40 minutes before sunrise or after sunset. The sunlight has gone and the sky takes on a deep, serene blue. In this window, not only does the sky gain depth instead of falling into total blackness; the absence of the sun also spares the image the glare of highlights and the pitch of shadows, so that every scene looks soft; and finally, the cool blue of the sky and the warm yellow of the city form a natural contrast, yielding rich color tension with no need for embellishment.

Nearly every photography enthusiast knows this. It is also what I taught my club members over and over.

Needless to say, the director knows it well too. A great deal of the film was shot during the blue hour, including the most famous song-and-dance sequence, *A Lovely Night*. It is not only the first high point of the two’s relationship but also the scene from which the film poster was made.

The blue hour is perfect for photography in every way except one: it passes.

The 40 minutes mentioned above is in fact an optimistic figure. In practice the blue hour’s duration varies with latitude and season, and sometimes lasts only 15 to 30 minutes.

Precisely because of this, when the production chose to rely entirely on natural light as its key light, they knew the challenge they faced. The window each day allowed them only 4 takes, each a single continuous shot.

In the end they shot over two evenings and used the last take of the second. By that take the light was already a little darker even than expected, but the emotional tension of the two moving from bickering to dancing, the tap steps, and the camera blocking had all reached their most perfect state.

But this, after all, is only a problem of filmmaking. If two days were not enough, they could come back for a third. Mia and Seb’s relationship, however, could not be extended by even a single day.

Like that dreamlike blue, purple, and pink, their relationship was fleeting in the end. You always know that night comes after the blue hour, unavoidably, beyond recovery. And it is precisely the speed with which it vanishes that keeps it in your memory, and makes it so immeasurably beautiful.

What comes after the night is not nothing but a sky full of stars. Los Angeles is a city of stars. Here there are not only dazzling celebrities but thousands upon thousands of ordinary people who may be chasing dreams or may not be, yet are still working hard at living. You and I are all stars.

After Mia and Seb parted, neither was worse off. One realized a dream of film, the other a dream of music. One found a fulfilling family life, the other a full inner life. This at least proves their choice back then was not wrong.

Whether that choice was “right”, there is no single answer.

We have all become shining stars in the pitch-black sky, but without that blue-hour sky, how am I to find you again among the stars?

Before the winter break ended, she and I found a chance to go out together.

We called it going out, though neither of us knew what to do with ourselves. We ended up in a bookstore inside a shopping mall, each bought a book, then sat facing each other at one table and started to read.

I do not remember whether I looked at her across the table. Perhaps I did, or I would not remember her face from that day so clearly. Otherwise we were both well behaved, quietly finishing our own books.

Coming out of the bookstore we went to eat. While we waited for the food, without asking whether I agreed, she took my phone. It was password-locked, of course, but she guessed the password at once and began browsing.

I neither agreed nor objected; I just sat there and let her. To be honest, for several minutes I was astonished that she could guess my password outright, and that she rifled through my phone so unscrupulously, unafraid of running into something she should not see.

Then again, there really was nothing in my phone that should not be seen. I am not the sort to write secret love letters on my phone.

When the meal was done we wandered aimlessly, just killing time until we had to part. At the end, she suddenly told me that she had invented an excuse to give her family for going out alone with me today.

I said nothing in particular. Neither did she.

At last I said goodbye to her and walked out the mall’s main entrance; it was already dusk. The small town in deep winter was still cold, a hint of pink had begun to spread at the edge of the sky, and the lights had come on. I called a taxi to take me home.

That was the last time I saw her in real life.

# Epilogue

On 14 February 2026, *La La Land* was re-released in mainland China.

I bought a ticket in the highest projection format I could get. Still this small town, still this season, even still the same shopping mall.

Even though I can recite every shot of this film by now, I could not possibly miss a chance to see it on the big screen. Call it academic research.

All the more so because this was the first time I had seen my favorite film on the big screen.

So, thank you.

I do not want to deny my own experience. Not every time, but there are always a few times when I rewatch it and think of you, of those days.

Many things after this film may have nothing to do with you, but in those winter days years ago, your influence on me was genuinely great. If I did not acknowledge that, I could never watch *La La Land* properly for the rest of my life.

Nor do I need to agonize over the ending of this little story between us any longer. I used to be very sad about it; now I am not.

I have become a star with a bit of light to it. Not bad.

May you be one too. May your light be dazzling, and may you find another star to keep you company.

And if *La La Land* is re-released again, remember to go and see it.

Not for anyone’s sake, not for any reason—just for the film itself.

After all, it really is a good film, isn’t it?
