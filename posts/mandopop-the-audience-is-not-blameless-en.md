We have grown so used to the sheer wretchedness of the Chinese pop music scene that it no longer surprises us. Calling out that wretchedness has, by now, become its own orthodoxy. We tread the muck called Mandopop, spittle spraying from our mouths, fighting by day, and by night grieving the endlessly deferred revival of Chinese-language pop. In this never-ending war, people who know full well it’s futile keep throwing themselves in, while the audience seems to have become the supreme arbiter. They convict everyone else and leave themselves off the charge sheet. The moment anyone dares point this out, the response is swift and merciless: *The audience has done nothing wrong; it’s the entire industry that’s to blame!* Even the bravest would keep their distance from that barrage. And yet, for all the absolution granted to our esteemed listeners—who, day after day, rain blows on everyone in sight—the industry has not improved one bit. When things have come to such a pass, how can we not question the supposedly self-evident axiom: “the audience is innocent”?

I don’t intend to spend a whole essay proving *why* the audience is not innocent. Many have tried before, and most have come to little good. The standard arguments on my side have grown so familiar to the other camp that they’ve lost most of their force. We may have to admit that the divide between those who believe the audience innocent and those who don’t is simply too wide to be bridged by yet another lengthy debate. That said, there is one thing I can still do: as someone firmly convinced that the audience is not innocent, I can point to which groups of listeners strike me as least innocent of all. In other words, I’m not going to force readers to share my conclusion—I’m just going to present some examples. Whether these examples are innocent—and, if they are not, whether that is enough to show, at least in part, that criticizing the audience is necessary?

# I. Worship the Old, Scorn the New—at Any Age

First in line are those who worship the past and disparage the present. I say “at any age” because this tendency is by no means limited to older listeners; if anything, the peculiar ecosystem of Chinese pop has made it even more pronounced among young people, outstripping their elders. To be fair, the reason listeners of all generations so uniformly believe that “older Chinese music was simply better” ultimately stems from the failure of the new generation to deliver. Among the newer acts who have managed to go mainstream, none can hold a candle to the kings and queens of yesteryear. Meanwhile, those with talent and originality struggle to gain commercial recognition. But none of that justifies the sheer shamelessness with which so many listeners indulge in their nostalgia.

How far do these past-worshippers go? Their breadth of listening is likely the thinnest of any group—yet they are among the most eager to pontificate and play the critic. How so? I happen to have a perfect example. The Bilibili creator @HOPICO runs one of the best independent music media outlets on the Chinese internet, especially since launching the HMA (HOPICO Music Awards). Obviously, the award honors each year’s Chinese-language albums. Just as viewers were delighting in discovering so many treasured musicians, in shuffled the nostalgia brigade—young, middle-aged, old alike—trailing the rotten stench of the grave, not even caring that green fluid was already seeping from the corners of their mouths as they struggled to raise their crumbling skeletal arms and jab their fingers:

> “A Chinese Top 10 is already hard to scrape together. Picking the forty best albums is truly impressive.”
>
> “It feels like it’s gone off the rails from pop music. I don’t know a single one of these albums or artists, and haven’t listened to any of them either.”
>
> “Why isn’t pop music popular?!”
>
> “Honestly I’m only here for HOPICO’s video editing and Zhou Yang. I don’t know any of these nominees or winners, and I’m not going to listen to them either. I’m past the age of listening to ‘new songs’ and don’t really care to learn.”
>
> “What am I supposed to do—even I find it so niche! Doesn’t even meet pop standards.”
>
> “What’s the point if they’re all niche albums?”
>
> “Basically a niche-music try-hard—an alternative-pop hangout.”

(All of the above are quoted verbatim from the comment sections of HOPICO’s two videos on the 2025 HMA. Zhou Yang is the host of HOPICO.)

Some go even further, openly sneering: “Honestly, not as good as…” followed by the name of a Taiwanese pop star who blew up in the 2000s.

Can listeners like these help the Chinese-language music industry develop? Shouldn’t people who post comments like this be driven out?

The eras these worshippers hold dearest are nothing but the Cantopop and Mandopop waves of the 1970s and 1980s, or the millennial R&B boom of the 1990s and 2000s. But whichever era they revere, they all seem oblivious to the fact that this is merely their personal taste—taste that cannot be used to instruct anyone. The HMA spans pop, alternative, R&B, hip-hop, rock, jazz, electronic, and more. How did a batch of pop musicians who did fairly well decades ago give these listeners such complete confidence to look down on—and lecture, with the sour air of a pedantic scholar—newcomers they haven’t even heard? The genres don’t even line up. Were those kings and queens musical polymaths?

Of course, a crueler truth: these past-worshippers probably don’t even know what “genre” means.

Older Chinese pop is genuinely wonderful; I love it too. But people have to look ahead eventually. The very people who love to drone on about how “the Chinese music scene is regressing instead of progressing” are themselves the most wedded to the outworn, the most resistant to growth, the most thoroughly ossified of all.

I have nothing more to say.

# II. Lyrics First, the Music Anything Goes

Second are those who listen only to the lyrics and couldn’t care less about the music itself, no matter how shoddily it’s put together.

Sadly, though, even I am unwilling to say anything too harsh on this point, because this stems fundamentally from the national and cultural character of the Chinese people.

The Chinese have long excelled at creating and savoring the beauty of poetry and verse. Even in the case of Song *ci* and Yuan *qu*, what survives today is the text, not the musical scores that once accompanied them (I’m not saying this cultural trait *caused* the scores to be lost, of course). Perhaps driven by this historical inertia, the Chinese are especially adept at treating music as a form of literature, exalting works that expend great effort on the words rather than on musicality.

What has this led to? China was already a latecomer to modern popular music, and the demand for lyrical aesthetics has held its development hostage. I’m not saying that music with good lyrics has no value. I’m saying that for a very long time, we haven’t truly prioritized the development of music *as music*: the development of composition, arrangement, production, theory, and so on. So when Western pop entered the Chinese market and began its conquest, Chinese pop was naturally helpless to resist. You may love the beauty of words, but the ear is not an organ for reading. If people are so passionate about crafting tear-jerking lines, I suggest they go write modern poetry. Why bother with music at all?

Let me be clear: I’m not a Western-centrist. The unfortunate reality is that the pop music of our world today is built on Western music theory as the foundation of its edifice. I have no doubt that, at some ripe moment in the future, China will also be able to bloom with its own flower of pop music—one that holds lyrical beauty as its highest standard. But listeners’ current expectation for Mandopop is, evidently, an arm-wrestling match with Western pop, so my vision isn’t realistic yet. As long as the goal is still to do what Japan once did—catch up and then surpass—we have no choice but to take the music itself seriously, instead of staring at the lyrics all day.

The exaltation of lyrics has also spawned a wave of counterfeits. Far too much pop music is garbage from compositional logic to production quality, yet it gets embraced as the darling of the scene simply because the lyrics are written in classical Chinese, or semi-classical Chinese, or because one or two tracks of traditional Chinese instruments are used, or because two phrases of the major pentatonic scale are used. This is especially severe in hip-hop too: let a rapper say something about society and politics, and he is instantly elevated to the status of Lu Xun, regardless of how weak his flow and the music actually are by the standards of the genre. It is precisely this indifference to the music itself, this absence of genuine musical judgment, that allows mediocrity to pass for substance.

# III. Persecution-Complex “I’m Just a Commoner”

The third group I want to call out overlaps considerably with the first.

These people suffer from a widespread persecution complex. The symptoms: you’re having a discussion about music; you mention a concept they don’t understand, or simply name an album they’ve never heard; and they instantly trigger their defense mechanisms:

> “Oh, here comes Mr. Niche.”
>
> “Superiority complex off the charts.”
>
> “The masses love it—who the hell do you think you are?”

Then they turn around and accuse you of aesthetic bullying.

If the past-worshippers never look down at what comes after them, these people never look up at what stands above them. A perfectly ordinary exchange about music, and they feel personally attacked. When in doubt, they play the humble card—*I’m just an ordinary person who doesn’t know anything*—and throw themselves to the ground, covering their ears. It’s a sordid version of “I was but a commoner from Huaiyou.”

But who is persecuting them, really? It all stems from a deep-seated insecurity. They are indeed lacking in knowledge and narrow in perspective; they can’t follow the references other people make. But unwilling to concede any ground, they dismiss everything the other person says—including things that are common knowledge or must-listens within music circles—as nothing but a source of superiority.

These people aren’t here to listen to music. They’re here to pick a fight. I don’t understand why someone bringing up something you don’t know counts as “bullying”. By that logic, a primary school teacher teaching addition, subtraction, multiplication and division is guilty of “knowledge bullying”, and parents teaching life lessons are guilty of “experience bullying”.

The point is simple: no one can stop you from growing except yourself. Why not treat these moments as opportunities—to take in something new, to learn something you didn’t know? No one is born knowing much, but there will always be someone who knows more than you. So why not try to learn a little more, even if only to have more to talk about over tea? Why be so thin-skinned and quick to take offense?

Dear readers, ask yourselves again: are listeners like these truly innocent?

# IV. I’m Just Fretting in Vain

After all that, I can’t rule out one possibility: every criticism and concern I’ve raised is meaningless, and I’m simply fretting in vain.

In fact, not that many people care about the development of Mandopop; they just see everyone else saying it and join in to trample on it. Not that many people want to encounter new things or become better versions of themselves; to them, listening to music is the most trivial of pastimes. The Chinese music scene is far from thriving, but it hasn’t been declared dead either, and the talented musicians I know can still put food on the table.

But I believe that we all absorb an endless stream of input every day, and eventually something has to come back out. I have spent eight years with music, and there are things I simply am not willing to let slide. If I too keep silent, then I too must bear the responsibility and the cost of that silence. In this world, there is no such thing as a true bystander.

Thank you for reading this far.
